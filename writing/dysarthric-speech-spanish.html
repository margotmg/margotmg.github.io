When the microphone cannot hear you

Voice interfaces promise independence to people with limited mobility. They work worst for many of the people who would gain most. We tried to close that gap for three Spanish speakers with dysarthria.

Speech recognition is the obvious interface for someone who cannot easily use their hands. Environmental control systems, virtual assistants, voice dictation: all of it assumes a microphone is easier than a keyboard.

Dysarthria breaks that assumption. It is a motor speech disorder caused by disturbed neuromuscular control. It does not affect a person’s grammar or what they intend to say; it affects articulation. And articulation is precisely what a recogniser is listening for.

The circularity is cruel. Systems are trained on large speech corpora; large corpora are hard to record from people who find speaking effortful; so the systems are not trained on them; so they do not work for them. For Spanish specifically, there were no dysarthric speech databases at all. Those that exist elsewhere are mostly English, and were collected for clinical diagnosis rather than for training a recogniser.

Four speakers, at home

We worked with three speakers with dysarthria and one control. C1 was a 13-year-old girl with congenital cerebral palsy, with a slow rate, breathy voice and long syllables. M1 was a 51-year-old man, also with congenital cerebral palsy, slurred and somewhat nasal. W1 was a 59-year-old woman whose dysarthria followed brain anoxia, with short speech and unclear vowels. R1, aged 26 with no speech pathology, gave us a ceiling to measure against.

Each recorded 21 commands across 10 separate sessions, giving 210 clips per speaker. Two details there mattered. We shuffled the command order between sessions so the system could not learn a sequence. And everything was recorded at home, on the speakers’ own schedule, rather than in a lab: the recogniser should be adapted to the room it will actually be used in, and the speakers should not have to travel to be studied.

Four ways to adapt a model

We used Microsoft Azure’s Custom Speech to Text, which allows a speaker-independent model to be adapted on a small amount of someone’s own speech. Training used leave-one-out cross-validation: nine sessions in, the tenth held back for testing.

Both kinds of help mattered, and they compounded. Restricting the vocabulary helps because the system no longer has to consider every Spanish word, only the commands that exist. Acoustic adaptation helps because the model learns this speaker’s particular articulation. Together they beat either alone, for every speaker.

But the improvement was not even. After training, M1 reached the highest accuracy, then C1, with W1 lowest. The same procedure, the same amount of data, very different outcomes. That is because dysarthria is not one condition with one severity. Any claim that a technique “works for dysarthric speech” should be read with that in mind.

The machines and the humans agreed

We also ran a perceptual test: 85 listeners transcribed 30 commands from each speaker and rated how hard each was to understand, from 1 to 5. Ten of the listeners had experience talking with people with dysarthria; that experience made no detectable difference to their scores.

The two measures move together: the speaker listeners found hardest is the one they transcribed worst. That is a useful validation: the difficulty rating is not a vague impression; it corresponds to a measurable failure to understand.

It also sets the comparison honestly. The adapted models were not merely better than the stock model; for these speakers and these commands they outperformed a room of human listeners. The bar that matters is not “as good as typical speech”. It is “better than the understanding this person currently gets”.

Then we put it online

Finally we built a live site where the speakers used their own trained model through a microphone, in real time, with no vocabulary restriction, since restricting it is not practical with live input. Accuracy dropped for all three. There was also no longer any “not recognised” outcome: given live audio the system always returns something, right or wrong.

That last detail is the one I keep returning to. A system that fails silently tells you it failed. A system that always answers does not. For someone relying on voice to control their environment, a confident wrong answer is a worse failure mode than no answer at all, and it is the failure mode that live deployment produces.

This was a pilot with three speakers. It is not a general result and should not be read as one. What it does show is that personal adaptation is worth the effort: the gap between a stock recogniser and one adapted to an individual is large enough to matter in daily use, and the recording burden (210 short commands, at home, spread over weeks) is small enough to be realistic.

Based on: Margot Madina, Eva Navas and Inma Hernáez, “Evaluation of STT Technology Performance for Spanish Dysarthric Speech: Description of a Pilot Experiment”, ICCHP-AAATE 2022 Open Access Compendium, Linz. Read the paper.

The paper labels the third speaker F1 in its participant description and W1 throughout its results; W1 is used here for consistency with the reported figures.
