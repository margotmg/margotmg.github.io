[01] AI and Accessibility LREC-COLING 2024

Can ChatGPT write Easy-to-Read Spanish?

We gave it ten news articles, the official rulebook, and ten readers who actually depend on Easy-to-Read. Every one of them preferred the version a human had written.

Roughly 16% of the world’s adults lack literacy skills, around 759 million people. After the pandemic, the number of children struggling with reading rose from 460 million to 584 million. These are not edge cases. They describe a substantial share of the people any public text is written for.

Easy-to-Read (E2R) is the response: a controlled variant of a language, with its own vocabulary, syntax and layout rules, designed so that a document can actually be understood. In Spanish it is called Lectura Fácil (LF), and since 2018 it has had a formal standard, UNE 153101:2018 EX, which runs to 130 rules.

It is also slow and expensive to produce. The standard describes a two-phase process: an adaptor rewrites the document with a designer, then a group of validators (people with reading comprehension difficulties) read it and report what they could not follow. The draft goes back and round again until they confirm it is comprehensible. That loop is the reason E2R texts are scarce, and it is the reason an automatic shortcut is tempting.

So we tested one. If a large language model could do even part of this work, it would matter. The question was not whether ChatGPT could produce something simpler, which it plainly can, but whether what it produces is Easy-to-Read in the sense the standard and its readers mean.

What we did

We took ten Spanish news texts from 2023 published by Irekia, the Basque Government’s open-government channel. These were chosen for a specific reason: Irekia already publishes official Lectura Fácil versions of some of its articles. That gave us a human-made gold standard to compare against, rather than only a before and after.

We wrote a prompt in Spanish that first supplied the UNE rules, then asked for the adaptation. We used 85 of the standard’s 130 rules: the ones covering orthotypography, vocabulary, sentences, text organisation and document presentation. We set aside those about images and paratextual elements, which a text-only model cannot act on. We deliberately used the public web interface rather than the API, because that is how most people actually use these tools.

Automatic

MultiAzterTest, a tool measuring 125+ features of cohesion, language and readability, used to compare all three versions of each text.

Checklist

Two native Spanish evaluators scored each text against a capability checklist. We added three new capabilities covering layout, which existing checklists ignore.

Readers

Ten people with a recognised disability between 33% and 79% read one version, retold it, answered questions, then chose which they preferred.

It shortens. Aggressively.

The clearest result is the bluntest. ChatGPT’s output was the shortest version of nine out of ten texts, and for the tenth it came in just one word longer than the human adaptation. Text 8 went from 1,143 words to 250. That is not simplification; it is summarisation, and the two are not the same thing.

Losing that much content matters, because in E2R the adaptor is not only allowed to add material but expected to. Explanations, examples and definitions are what make a text reachable. Summarising strips exactly the scaffolding a reader needs.

Simpler words, harder sentences

The automatic analysis found a genuine strength: ChatGPT used the fewest rare words of any version. Its lexical simplification is good. But the same analysis showed its sentences were longer than the human versions, with more subordinate clauses and more propositions per sentence.

That combination is awkward. The model swaps hard words for easy ones, which is visible and easy to check, while leaving the syntax dense. And syntactic complexity is precisely what makes a sentence hard to hold in working memory. A text can pass a vocabulary check and still be unreadable for the person it was adapted for.

Every text landed in the moderate band or below. Not one reached “substantial”. The two evaluators agreed strongly (a κ of 0.85 is near-perfect), so this is not noise in the scoring. Where they did disagree, it was most often about simplicity, which is the hardest of the criteria to judge from the page.

The qualitative failures were more revealing than the scores. For seven of the ten texts, ChatGPT invented subsections with subtitles that the standard does not call for. Worse, it sometimes put information only in a subtitle: one text carried the heading “Why did On-giz win the prize?” when no prize had been mentioned anywhere in the body. One text came back as a list of enumerated ideas rather than continuous prose. The register drifted informal. Sentences were split across lines in ways the rules explicitly forbid.

The grammar was fine. That was never the problem.

Then we asked the readers

This is the part that academic evaluation usually skips. Ten people with cognitive disabilities each read one version and were asked to retell it and answer questions about it. Then they were shown both and asked which they preferred.

Unanimous. And their reasons were concrete, not vague preference: they understood more of the words; the line spacing was bigger; the sentences were shorter; things were better explained; the human version simply contained more content and more explanation.

That last reason is worth sitting with. The model’s most obvious behaviour, cutting length, is the one readers named as a reason for preferring the alternative. One participant said the ChatGPT version was easier, but still chose the human one, because of how the ideas were organised and explained.

What this means

ChatGPT is not useless here. Its lexical simplification is real and could genuinely assist an adaptor. But it does not produce Easy-to-Read text. It over-summarises, it will not reliably add the explanatory material that defines the genre, and it does not follow the rules it was handed even when they are supplied directly in the prompt.

There is a methodological point underneath this too. Had we stopped at the automatic metrics, ChatGPT would have looked promising: shorter texts, fewer rare words, clean grammar. The checklist complicated that. The readers settled it. For accessibility work, evaluation that never reaches the intended audience is not evaluation; it is a proxy, and proxies fail in exactly the direction that flatters the system.

Which is the broader worry. Most people using these tools are not in a position to audit them. An output that reads fluently and arrives in two seconds carries an authority it has not earned. For text whose entire purpose is to be understood by someone who is otherwise excluded, that gap between appearing competent and being competent is not an academic concern.
